4.3. Conflict Test Results

Table 2 reports the conflict test defined in Section 2. A model attending to the voice should score similarly on both arms, whereas a
model following the words should invert and fall below chance on
the conflict arm. A model reading affect from prosody would also
invert, and the transcript-only baseline below shows that the words
alone suffice. No such test is possible for PD, since the words are
fixed by the protocol. Results in Table 2 show a strong language bias.
On the depression segments four of the six models fall below chance
when the words point away from the label, while every model except
Audio Flamingo 2 scores above 0.80 when the words and the label
agree. Speaker-level bootstrap intervals for the difference between
the two arms exclude zero for all six models on depression and for
the three Qwen models on Pitt. On the same pairs the transcript alone
scores 0.15 on conflict and 0.96 on agreement for Qwen2.5-Omni and
0.22 and 0.95 for Qwen3-Omni, and for Qwen2.5-Omni the transcript
decision matches the audio decision on 86 percent of clips. On the
Pitt dataset the transcript alone shows the same arm gap as the audio
(0.56 on conflict, 0.71 on agreement). The conflict arm stays below
chance under the two other prompt wordings, at 0.34 and 0.24, and
under sentiment thresholds of 0.60 and 0.80 (646 and 281 pairs), at
0.26 and 0.19. Replacing the severity gates of 15 and 4 with a PHQ-8
cut at 10 gives 924 pairs from 237 speakers, on which the conflict
arm scores 0.18. A second sentiment scorer (DistilBERT fine-tuned
on SST-2) selects 1,334 pairs, on which the conflict arm scores 0.46
[0.42, 0.51], at chance rather than below it, and stays 0.39 below
the agreement arm. The inversion holds on the 420 segments both
scorers call conflicting (0.20) and not on the 855 the first scorer calls
neutral (0.62). Audio Flamingo 2 is the only model whose audio
encoder, CLAP [29], was not trained for speech recognition, so its
transcription is limited. It also answers Yes on 97 percent or more
of the clips, and Qwen2-Audio on 87 percent or more, so their AUC
reflects ranking rather than decisions. On the Pitt dataset the three
Qwen models drop by 0.17 to 0.29 from the agreement to the conflict
arm but none falls significantly below chance, Kimi-Audio shows
no drop, and the two Audio Flamingo models score 0.53 to 0.61 on
both arms (Table 2). Asking Qwen2.5-Omni to judge the voice alone
moves its Pitt answer by −0.01 [−0.04, 0.02].
4.4. Supervised Fine-tuning
To narrow the gap, we retrain the projector between the audio encoder
and the language model so that paralinguistic information becomes
more salient to the language model. After fine-tuning the projector,

AUC improves significantly on every dataset except MDVR-KCL,
which has 37 speakers, and E-DAIC, which does not move (Table 1).
On the Pitt dataset the retrained projector reaches 0.77, the level of
the answer-state probe of Section 4.2, and 0.74 to 0.81 across three
further seeds. Its conflict arm stays at chance in every run (0.45 to
0.57, every interval including 0.5) while its agreement arm rises to
0.82 to 0.90, so the projector recovers the overall AUC through the
arm the words already help. A class-balanced loss does not change
this. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [30] of the language model (rank 8,
all attention projections of the 28 layers, 5.0 M trainable parameters
against 4.6 M for the projector) reaches 0.825 on Pitt, 0.06 [0.02,
0.10] above the projector run, and 0.55 on its conflict segments, and
training both together gives 0.77. On E-DAIC the retrained projector
stays at the zero-shot level (+0.02 [−0.01, 0.05], and 0.82 with a
second seed), consistent with a task the words carry more than the
voice. Retraining it on the E-DAIC pairs themselves lifts the conflict
arm from 0.22 to 0.59 and lowers agreement from 0.96 to 0.67, so
the language bias can be trained away only at the cost of the arm
the words were carrying. On Parkinson’s speech the deficit sits in
how the representation is read out rather than in the representation,
since the signal is present at every depth and retraining the projector
recovers it. On Alzheimer’s what is recovered is at least partly lexical.
The fine tuning is on Qwen2.5-Omni, and we release the segment
lists, fold assignments, scripts and per-clip answer scores at https:
//github.com/parw8649/audio-llm-utility-gap.
5. CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we showed how Audio LLMs could underperform when
diagnosing three different clinical conditions. We then inspected
the audio representations through probing to learn whether the information necessary for a correct diagnosis is present. The probing
results demonstrated a wide utility gap, i.e., the LLM does not effectively use the information available from the audio encoder, hence,
resulting in lower performance. Similar to previous work [7], we
found a strong language bias in Audio LLMs as they are more likely
to follow the spoken words rather than paralinguistic speech. To
alleviate this problem and narrow the utility gap, we performed a
supervised fine-tuning limited to the projector layer, keeping the rest
of the model’s weights frozen, resulting in a significant performance
gain in AD and PD detection, but not in MDD, where the answer
already follows the words. This work demonstrated that the hidden
state representations carry the necessary paralinguistic information,
and future work should focus on further narrowing the utility gap.
